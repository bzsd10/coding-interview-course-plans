# Online Coding Interview Course: How to Choose One Before You Pay (Plans, Prices, Time Commitment and Coverage Gaps)

Most people typing "online coding interview course" into a search bar aren't looking for information. They already know they need to prepare. What they don't know is which format matches the number of weeks they actually have left.

That mismatch is where money gets wasted. Buying a problem bank with eight weeks on the clock and no plan produces roughly the same outcome as buying nothing. Paying bootcamp prices when you already solve mediums comfortably is overkill you'll feel every month for a year.

So this is a buying guide rather than a list. Below: how the four common formats differ, which one to pick based on your timeline, and where AlgoMonster — the pattern-first course written by former Google engineers — sits relative to the alternatives. That includes its current plans and prices, plus the parts of interview prep it deliberately leaves out.

## Start with your timeline, not the course

Three things decide almost everything about which course makes sense:

1. **How many weeks until your loop.** Under six weeks changes the answer completely. Not because shortcuts exist, but because volume-based prep needs more runway than structure-based prep.
2. **Whether your bottleneck is writing code or choosing an approach.** If you can implement a solution once you know what it is, your gap is pattern recognition. If you can't implement it, no pattern course will save you — that's a fundamentals problem.
3. **Whether you're paying once or subscribing.** A yearly subscription you forget to cancel costs more than a lifetime purchase you use twice.

Answer those honestly and the field narrows to one or two options. Skip them and you'll end up doing what most candidates do: buying the biggest problem bank available and grinding randomly until the week before the interview.

## The four formats you'll actually find

Almost every paid option on the market fits one of these buckets.

| Format | What it teaches | Where it breaks down | Typical price range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Problem bank (LeetCode and similar) | Volume practice, speed, company tags | No curriculum, no explanation of *why* an approach fits | Free tier plus ~$35/month or ~$159/year for premium |
| Video course | Walkthroughs of individual problems | Passive; you watch someone else reason instead of doing it | ~$99–$299/year |
| Structured pattern course | Reusable templates and when to apply them | Smaller problem set; needs volume practice alongside | One-time or annual, usually under $200 |
| Live bootcamp | Cohort, mentorship, mock interviews | Cost; often scheduled around someone else's timeline | $7,000 and up |

The differences that matter aren't about content quantity. They're about what happens when you hit a problem you've never seen. A problem bank gives you a place to practice that moment. A video course shows you someone else's version of it. A pattern course tries to teach the decision itself — which data structure, which traversal, which of the twenty-odd recurring shapes the problem belongs to.

That last category is the one AlgoMonster occupies.

## Why "how many problems" is the wrong question

Every comparison page leads with problem counts, which is the least useful number on the page. LeetCode lists thousands; AlgoMonster's catalog is in the low hundreds. Neither figure tells you whether you'll pass.

What actually determines whether you freeze in a 45-minute screen is how long you spend not knowing where to start. Candidates who fail usually don't fail at implementation. They spend fifteen minutes trying approaches that don't fit the problem's structure, then run out of time.

Pattern-based courses are built for that specific failure. The tradeoff is real, though: fewer problems means less raw repetition, and repetition is what makes a solution fast. The two formats are complements more than substitutes, and most people who get offers use both.

Pick **yearly** if you have a specific interview cycle in front of you and no plans to keep the habit up afterward. Twelve months is plenty to finish a curriculum designed for four to eight weeks of work, and you keep the option of walking away without feeling like you left money on the table.

Pick **lifetime** if any of these apply: you're early in your career and expect more interview loops, you're the type who reviews patterns before switching jobs, or you're mentoring people and want the material around. Engineers who job-hop every couple of years are the obvious case, since the alternative is whichever annual price exists at that point in the future.

## Where AlgoMonster stops

Nobody gets everything from one platform, and it's better to know the edges now than to discover them two weeks before an onsite.

**System design is introductory.** There's content on it, but not the depth you need for a senior loop. Engineers targeting L5 and above at large companies typically put 20–40 hours into distributed systems, database design, consistency models and scaling tradeoffs, and that has to come from somewhere else.

**Behavioral prep is thin.** Enough to know the format, not enough to run Leadership Principles or impact-driven storytelling drills. If your loop includes a dedicated behavioral round, budget separate time for it.

**There's no live interview pressure.** Every problem is solved at your own pace with nobody watching. Real screens have a clock, an engineer on the other side of the screen, and follow-up questions like *how does your complexity change if the data doesn't fit in memory?* Practicing that out loud is a different skill, and it needs a person or a mock-interview tool.

**No video, no mentors, no community of any real size.** Text works for readers. It's the wrong buy for people who need someone explaining on a whiteboard.

**Fewer problems than a dedicated bank.** If you've already internalized the patterns and just need reps, you're paying for material you don't need.

## A six-week plan that uses the course properly

The curriculum is designed to be finished in four to eight weeks at one to two hours a day. Here's how that shakes out with volume practice layered in.

**Weeks 1–2.** Work the core patterns in order. Slow down on the reasoning sections — the point of week one is understanding why two pointers fits a sorted-array problem and why it doesn't fit an unsorted one. Aim to finish a pattern and its problems, not to set speed records.

**Weeks 3–4.** Continue through graphs, dynamic programming, and the heavier categories, and start interleaving. Solve one pattern problem, then one unseen problem from a bank, on the same day. Interleaving is what proves you're recognizing patterns rather than remembering the last lesson you read.

**Week 5.** Switch the balance. Mostly volume practice, using Speedrun for review passes over the patterns that felt shakiest. Target the company question lists if you know where you're interviewing.

**Week 6.** No new material. Mock interviews with another person, out loud, on a timer, with follow-up questions you don't get to rehearse. This is the week AlgoMonster can't cover for you, and it's the week that decides whether the previous five were enough.

Six weeks is tight for someone starting from fundamentals — that's genuinely a three-month project. It's workable for someone who can already write correct code and needs the selection skill.
